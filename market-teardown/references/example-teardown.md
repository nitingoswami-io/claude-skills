# Nobody owns the 1–6 month furnished flat in India. There is a reason.

Every week, a few hundred professionals land in Bengaluru with a joining date and nowhere to live. India's global capability centres are adding roughly 300,000 jobs a year, and Bengaluru alone accounts for 44% of all GCC office leasing (CBRE, Q4 2025). Each of those seats is a person who needs a furnished apartment for one to six months — too long for a hotel to make financial sense, too short for a landlord to write a lease.

India has no product for that window. Airbnb is built for leisure nights. NoBroker is built for 11-month bare-shell leases. Serviced apartments are hotel-priced at two to four times market rent and concentrated in a handful of micro-markets. The corporate share of India's serviced apartment market alone is about $1.05 billion (roughly ₹1,000 Cr), and the wider mid-term corporate stay opportunity sits somewhere between $1–2 billion, growing 8–15% a year.

Blueground has already proved the category works globally: $560 million revenue at roughly 35% gross margin. It has no India operations. That is validation and a clock at the same time.

Which is precisely why this deserves suspicion rather than enthusiasm. A billion-dollar gap that nobody has filled in a market with 30 million-user property portals is usually not an oversight. It is usually a structural refusal.

## The demand is real, and one critical number does not exist

The demand side holds up under scrutiny. India hosts 2,117 GCCs across 3,728 units, employing 2.36 million people and generating $98.4 billion in revenue (FY26, Zinnov–Nasscom). Headcount is tracking toward 3.4 million by 2030. India is now the world's fourth-largest business travel market at $38.3 billion a year. Return-to-office pushed rents in IT corridors up 20–30%, and Bengaluru absorbed around 300,000 migrants in 2025.

Layer in the fresher cohorts — GCCs alone are projected to create about 400,000 entry-level jobs by 2030, and Infosys is back to hiring 15,000–20,000 freshers a year — and the arrival volume is structural, not cyclical.

How corporates solve this today is the tell. They book hotel long-stays and serviced apartments at negotiated rates, two to four times bare rent. They lease flats as company guest houses, which attracts 18% GST under reverse charge with input tax credit generally blocked. Or they give the employee 15–30 days of temporary accommodation under the relocation policy and then leave them to it. Fragmented, expensive, nobody aggregating.

And here is the number that does not exist: there is no published figure anywhere for annual corporate relocations or deputations in India. Not from Nasscom, not from CBRE, not from the relocation management companies. That absence is not a research failure — it is a market signal. The buyer is real but has never been counted, which means nobody has ever sized this to a board.

## Airbnb structurally cannot take it, and that gap is the product

Airbnb's own disclosures show it retreating from this ground. Stays of 28 nights or more were about 21% of nights in 2022 and about 17% in 2024, and the Q1 2026 shareholder letter notes short-term growth now outpacing long-term. Airbnb for Work has seen no visible investment since 2019. India is one of Airbnb's fastest-growing markets at +50% nights year on year, but the push is Goa, Gen Z and spiritual tourism.

The blockers are concrete. Airbnb states plainly that it does not issue invoices for bookings — the host must, and most individual hosts sit under the ₹20 lakh registration threshold and are unregistered. So a corporate booker gets no GSTIN-bearing invoice and no input tax credit. That single fact disqualifies Airbnb from most Indian corporate travel desks before pricing is even discussed. A Deloitte study found only 9% of companies include non-hotel lodging in their corporate booking tools at all.

Then the India-specific ones. Police verification of tenants is mandatory in every metro within roughly 24 hours of move-in, with the landlord criminally liable for non-compliance, and Airbnb has no workflow for it. Resident welfare associations restrict short-stay churn through bylaws and NOCs, and courts have backed them — there have been active crackdowns on Airbnb-style operations in Noida high-rises.

That last constraint is the one worth staring at, because it inverts. RWAs tolerate verified long-term tenants. A long-stay-only platform with verification and RWA-facing documentation turns Airbnb's single largest India liability into a structural advantage. Same for pricing: monthly Airbnb stays run 20–50% above direct local rents globally, and wider in India, where a ₹35,000/month 2BHK lists at ₹4,000–6,000 a night.

## The graveyard says the same thing four times

This category has killed well-funded companies with remarkable consistency, and the pattern is identical every time.

- **NestAway (India):** raised over $110 million, peaked at a $220 million valuation with 35,000 tenants, sold in distress for about ₹90 Cr (~$11 million) in 2023 — a 95% haircut. Killed by a 15–22% take rate that could not absorb operations costs, informal rent guarantees against vacancy it did not control, and deposit-refund disputes that destroyed trust on both sides.
- **Zeus Living (US):** $150 million raised, with Airbnb itself as an investor. Master-leased homes with guaranteed rent. Shut down in November 2023 when it could not pay property owners.
- **Landing (US):** roughly $237 million raised, master-lease-heavy, near death in 2022–23 with landlord lawsuits over rent arrears. It survived only by killing the lease model — its Autopilot platform, where the owner keeps lease risk and Landing supplies demand and operations, became the exclusive model in October 2024 and attracted $180 million in fresh financing by May 2025.
- **OYO Life:** even OYO's capital could not make fixed-lease co-living survive a demand shock. Shut by March 2021.

The lesson, stated once: fixed obligations against variable demand is the death pattern. The survivors either run master leases with extreme discipline at premium price points, or push lease risk back to owners and sell demand, operations and trust instead.

The opposite extreme is not safe either. HousingAnywhere, the surviving pure marketplace, was EBITDA-profitable and still written down to roughly $36 million in 2025. Thin take rates on undifferentiated listings do not blow up; they just stay small.

## The economics, and who actually pays

The buyer is the corporate travel desk, HR mobility lead or admin head — not the employee, and not the homeowner. What they cannot buy today is a GST-compliant invoice against their GSTIN with consolidated monthly central billing for furnished monthly stays. That is the product, and everything else is packaging around it.

The unit sketch, directional: a furnished 2BHK on ORR or Whitefield at ₹45,000–55,000 a month, a 10% booking take of ₹4,500–5,500 per unit per month, plus ₹1,500–3,000 of attach revenue from managed operations, compliance paperwork and furnishing amortisation. At 300 occupied units that is roughly ₹2–2.5 Cr annualised. Small. The wedge is not the revenue; it is proof of corporate account retention and owner NPS before spending on a second city.

Revenue starts in month one, because the concierge version needs no product — a spreadsheet, WhatsApp, hand-picked owner flats and a chartered accountant issuing invoices proves the entire loop.

But the margin model is not yet knowable. The GST classification for this — residential dwelling versus accommodation service, 18% reverse charge on residential renting to registered entities, input tax credit blocking rules — sits on a litigated fault line that shifted as recently as September 2025. Whether the platform is merchant-of-record or facilitator changes the economics materially. That counsel memo comes before the first corporate pitch, not after.

## The verdict

**Conditional go — asset-light only, and the GST structure decides the margin before anything else does.**

Four conditions, all binding:

1. No master leases and no blanket rent guarantees, ever. The owner keeps vacancy risk. This is the only variable that separates Landing v2 from Zeus.
2. A tax counsel memo on the invoicing structure before the corporate pitch deck is written.
3. Escrowed deposits with an itemised deduction workflow and a defined refund SLA — the single trust failure that killed NestAway, and something neither Airbnb nor NoBroker offers.
4. 150–300 certified units across three or four micro-markets before approaching an anchor account. Below that, a mobility head cannot rely on you.

The risk most likely to kill it is not competition. It is operations cost creep eating an 8–12% take rate, which is exactly the NestAway trap. Every service must be productised and priced, and expansion must saturate a micro-market before opening a new one.

Worth naming plainly: this is an India operating business with India buyers and on-ground headcount. It is the opposite shape to build-in-India-sell-overseas, and it should only be taken on by someone who wants to run field operations.

## What I'd validate next

Ten to fifteen interviews with mobility and travel-desk heads at Bengaluru GCCs, asking annual relocation volume, current cost per night, and who signs. That number does not exist publicly, so it has to be built. Then a weekend scraping Airbnb monthly listings against NoBroker rents in the same micro-markets to quantify the arbitrage. Then five to ten manual placements before any code is written.
